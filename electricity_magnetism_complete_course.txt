Part 2: Closed Surface 
What is Closed Surface? 
A surface enclosing volume. 
Examples: Sphere, Cube. 

Electric Flux Through Closed Surface 
Notation: 
[ 
\boxed{ 
\oint \vec E\cdot d\vec A 
} 
] 
The circle means: Add flux over the complete closed surface. 

Part 3: Gauss's Theorem 
Statement 
The total electric flux through any closed surface equals the enclosed charge divided by 
permittivity of free space. 

Mathematical Form 
[ 
\boxed{ 
\oint \vec E\cdot d\vec A = \frac{Q}{\epsilon_0} 
} 
] 
This is called Gauss's Law. 

Meaning 
Only charge inside matters. 
Case 1: Charge Inside Surface 
       _______ 
      /    +Q   \ 
      \_________/ 
Flux exists: $\phi = Q / \epsilon_0$

Case 2: Charge Outside Surface 
      +Q 
          _______ 
        /         \ 
        \_________/ 
Net flux: $\boxed{0}$ because no charge is enclosed. 
 
Proof of Gauss's Law (Very Important PYQ) 
Step 1: Consider charge (Q) at center of sphere. 
             ↑ E 
         ↗         ↖ 
       ↗     Q      ↖ 
         ↘         ↙ 
              ↓ E 
       Spherical Surface 

Step 2: Electric field due to charge: 
[ 
E = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{Q}{r^2} 
] 
Step 3: Flux through area element: 
[ 
d\phi=E\cdot dA 
] 
Step 4: Integrate over whole sphere: 
[ 
\phi = \oint E\cdot dA = \frac{Q}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r^2} \oint dA 
] 
But $\oint dA=4\pi r^2$. Hence: 
[ 
\phi = \frac{Q}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r^2} (4\pi r^2) = \boxed{ \frac{Q}{\epsilon_0} } 
] 
Proved. 

Differential Form (Asked in Set 1) 
Using Divergence Theorem: 
[ 
\boxed{ 
\nabla\cdot\vec E = \frac{\rho}{\epsilon_0} 
} 
] 
This is Maxwell's First Equation.

Why Use Gauss's Law? 
Symmetry makes complex derivations extremely easy compared to Coulomb's Law. 

1. Electric Field Due to Infinite Line Charge 
Linear charge density: $\lambda = Q / L$

Diagram 
         Infinite Line Charge 
                │ 
                │ λ 
                │ 
          ╔══════════╗ 
          ║          ║ 
          ║Cylinder  ║ 
          ║          ║ 
          ╚══════════╝ 
             Radius r 

Step 1: Choose Gaussian Surface 
Due to cylindrical symmetry, choose a cylindrical Gaussian surface. 
Step 2: Calculate Flux 
Flux through end caps = 0 (since $\theta = 90^\circ$). Only curved surface contributes: 
[ 
\phi = E(2\pi rL) 
] 
Step 3: Apply Gauss's Law 
[ 
E(2\pi rL) = \frac{Q}{\epsilon_0} = \frac{\lambda L}{\epsilon_0} 
] 
Cancel L: 
[ 
\boxed{ 
E = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi\epsilon_0 r} 
} 
] 
Observation: $E\propto\frac1r$

2. Electric Field Due to Infinite Plane Sheet 
Surface charge density: $\sigma = Q / A$

Diagram 
       Infinite Sheet 
══════════════════════════ 
           ↑ E 
══════════════════════════ 
           ↓ E 

Step 1: Choose Gaussian Surface 
Use a cylindrical pillbox perpendicular to the sheet. 
Step 2: Flux Calculation 
Flux through curved surface = 0. Flux through both flat caps = $EA + EA = 2EA$. 
Step 3: Apply Gauss's Law 
[ 
2EA = \frac{Q}{\epsilon_0} = \frac{\sigma A}{\epsilon_0} 
] 
Cancel A: 
[ 
\boxed{ 
E = \frac{\sigma}{2\epsilon_0} 
} 
] 
Observation: Electric field does NOT depend on distance. 

3. Electric Field Due to Uniformly Charged Sphere 
Case 1: Outside Sphere ($r > R$) 
Behaves like point charge at center: 
[ 
\boxed{ 
E = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{Q}{r^2} 
} 
] 
Case 2: Inside Sphere ($r < R$) 
Using volume charge density $\rho = Q / (\frac{4}{3}\pi R^3)$: 
[ 
Q_{enc} = Q \frac{r^3}{R^3} 
] 
Applying Gauss's Law: $E(4\pi r^2) = Q_{enc} / \epsilon_0$
[ 
\boxed{ 
E = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{Qr}{R^3} 
} 
]

Chapter 4: Electric Potential and Potential Difference
===========================================================================

PYQ Connection 
✅ Set 1 – Electric Dipole Potential (10 Marks) 
✅ Set 2 – Potential at Centre of Square (5 Marks) 

Why Do We Need Electric Potential? 
Moving a charge in an electric field requires work, which is stored as electrostatic potential energy. 

Electric Potential Difference 
[ 
\boxed{ 
V_B-V_A=\frac{W}{q} 
} 
] 
Unit: Volt ($1V = 1J/C$) 

Electric Potential 
Work done in bringing a unit positive charge from infinity to a point. 

Potential Due to Point Charge 
[ 
V = -\int_{\infty}^{r} \vec E\cdot d\vec r 
] 
Substituting $E$: 
[ 
\boxed{ 
V = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{Q}{r} 
} 
] 
Observation: $V \propto \frac{1}{r}$

Potential Due to Several Charges (Scalar Sum) 
[ 
\boxed{ 
V = V_1+V_2+V_3+\cdots = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \sum \frac{q_i}{r_i} 
} 
] 

Relation Between Electric Field and Potential 
[ 
dW = qE\cdot dl \quad \implies \quad dV = -E\cdot dl 
] 
[ 
\boxed{ 
E = -\frac{dV}{dr} 
} 
] 
Vector form: 
[ 
\boxed{ 
\vec E = -\nabla V 
} 
] 
Physical Meaning: Electric field points in the direction of steepest decrease of potential. 

Equipotential Surface 
A surface where potential is same everywhere. 
Properties: 
1. Work done along the surface is zero ($W = 0$). 
2. Electric field is always perpendicular to the surface. 

Potential Energy of Two Charges 
[ 
\boxed{ 
U = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{q_1 q_2}{r} 
} 
]

Chapter 5: Electric Dipole (Most Important Long Question)
===========================================================================

PYQ Frequency 
✅ Set 1 Question 2(a) – 10 Marks 
✅ Set 3 Question 2(a) – 10 Marks 

What is an Electric Dipole? 
Two equal and opposite charges separated by a small distance. 

Dipole Moment 
Product of charge and separation. 
      -q ●──────● +q 
            2a 
          → p 
Direction: From negative to positive charge. 
[ 
\boxed{ 
\vec p = q(2a) 
} 
] 
Unit: $C\cdot m$

Electric Potential Due to Dipole (General Point $P(r,\theta)$) 
Binomial approximation for $r \gg a$: 
$r_+ = r - a\cos\theta$ and $r_- = r + a\cos\theta$
[ 
V = \frac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \left( \frac{1}{r_+} - \frac{1}{r_-} \right) 
] 
[ 
\boxed{ 
V = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{p\cos\theta}{r^2} 
} 
] 
Special Cases: 
* Axial ($\theta = 0^\circ$): $V_{\text{axial}} = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{p}{r^2}$
* Equatorial ($\theta = 90^\circ$): $V_{\text{equatorial}} = 0$

Electric Field Due to Dipole (using $\vec{E} = -\nabla V$) 
Radial field: $E_r = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{2p\cos\theta}{r^3}$
Tangential field: $E_\theta = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{p\sin\theta}{r^3}$
Resultant: 
[ 
\boxed{ 
E = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{p}{r^3}\sqrt{1+3\cos^2\theta} 
} 
] 
Special Cases: 
* Axial field: $E_{\text{axial}} = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{2p}{r^3}$
* Equatorial field: $E_{\text{equatorial}} = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{p}{r^3}$

Torque on Dipole in Uniform Field 
[ 
\boxed{ 
\vec\tau = \vec p \times \vec E 
} 
] 
Magnitude: $\tau = pE\sin\theta$

Potential Energy of Dipole 
[ 
\boxed{ 
U = -\vec p \cdot \vec E = -pE\cos\theta 
} 
] 
* Stable equilibrium: $\theta = 0^\circ$
* Unstable equilibrium: $\theta = 180^\circ$

Chapter 6: Dielectrics and Polarization
===========================================================================

What is a Dielectric? 
An insulating material that polarizes under an external electric field. 

Polarization Vector (P) 
Dipole moment per unit volume. 
[ 
\boxed{ 
P = \frac{p}{V} 
} 
] 
Unit: $C/m^2$

Relation Between Polarization and Surface Charge Density 
Induced surface charge is called bound charge ($\sigma_p$). 
[ 
\boxed{ 
\sigma_p = \vec P \cdot \hat n = P 
} 
] 

Electric Susceptibility ($\chi_e$) 
Measures ease of polarization: 
[ 
\boxed{ 
P = \epsilon_0 \chi_e E 
} 
] 

Dielectric Constant (K) 
[ 
\boxed{ 
K = \frac{\epsilon}{\epsilon_0} = 1 + \chi_e 
} 
] 

Electric Displacement Vector (D) 
Combines effects of free and bound charges: 
[ 
\boxed{ 
D = \epsilon_0 E + P = \epsilon E 
} 
] 

Gauss's Law in Dielectrics 
Only free charges contribute to D-flux: 
[ 
\boxed{ 
\oint \vec D \cdot d\vec A = Q_{\text{free}} 
} 
] 
Differential Form: $\nabla \cdot \vec D = \rho_{\text{free}}$

Capacitor with Dielectric (K) 
Capacitance always increases: $C' = KC_0$. 
* Case 1: Battery Connected (Voltage V is constant) 
  * $V' = V_0$
  * $C' = KC_0$
  * $Q' = KQ_0$
  * $E' = E_0$
  * $U' = KU_0$ (increases)
* Case 2: Battery Disconnected / Isolated (Charge Q is constant) 
  * $Q' = Q_0$
  * $C' = KC_0$
  * $V' = V_0 / K$ (decreases)
  * $E' = E_0 / K$ (decreases)
  * $U' = U_0 / K$ (decreases)

Chapter 7: Magnetic Field and Magnetic Force
===========================================================================

What is Magnetic Field (B)? 
Region around a moving charge or magnet where magnetic forces are felt. 
Unit: Tesla (T) or Gauss ($1T = 10^4 G$) 

Lorentz Force 
Total force on a charge moving through electric and magnetic fields: 
[ 
\boxed{ 
\vec F = q(\vec E + \vec v \times \vec B) 
} 
] 
Only Magnetic field: $F = qvB\sin\theta$
* Parallel motion ($\theta = 0^\circ$): $F = 0$ (Straight path) 
* Perpendicular motion ($\theta = 90^\circ$): $F = qvB$ (Circular path) 

Radius of Circular Path (Centripetal = Magnetic force) 
[ 
\frac{mv^2}{r} = qvB \quad \implies \quad \boxed{ r = \frac{mv}{qB} } 
] 

Time Period of Revolution 
[ 
\boxed{ 
T = \frac{2\pi m}{qB} 
} 
] 
Observation: Time period is independent of speed and radius! 

Force on Current-Carrying Conductor 
[ 
\boxed{ 
\vec F = I(\vec L \times \vec B) 
} 
] 
Magnitude: $F = BIL\sin\theta$

Chapter 10: Ampere's Circuital Law
===========================================================================

Ampere's Circuital Law 
The line integral of magnetic field around any closed path equals $\mu_0$ times the total current enclosed. 
[ 
\boxed{ 
\oint \vec B \cdot d\vec l = \mu_0 I_{\text{enclosed}} 
} 
] 

Application 1: Infinite Straight Wire 
Circular Amperian loop of radius r: 
[ 
B(2\pi r) = \mu_0 I \quad \implies \quad \boxed{ B = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r} } 
] 

Application 2: Magnetic Field Inside a Long Solenoid 
Rectangular Amperian loop where only the inside parallel section contributes: 
[ 
Bl = \mu_0 N_{\text{enc}} I = \mu_0 (nl) I 
] 
[ 
\boxed{ 
B = \mu_0 n I 
} 
] 
where $n = N / L$ is the turns per unit length. 

Application 3: Magnetic Field Inside Toroid 
A toroid is a hollow circular ring on which a large number of insulated turns of wire are closely wound. It is essentially a solenoid bent into a closed circular shape.

Diagram 
           Cross-Section of Toroid
                . - ~ ~ ~ - .
            . '    \  │  /    ' .
          . '   ←   ●───●   →   ' .
         . '        │ c │        ' .
         │   ↑   ●───┼───●   ↑   │  r (mean radius)
         . .        │   │        . .
          . '   ←   ●───●   →   ' .
            . '    /  │  \    ' .
                ' - _ _ _ - '

Derivation 
Step 1: Choose a circular Amperian loop of radius $r$ inside the core of the toroid. By symmetry, the magnetic field $\vec{B}$ is constant in magnitude and tangent to the circular loop at all points.
Step 2: Calculate the line integral of $\vec{B}$:
[ 
\oint \vec B \cdot d\vec l = B \oint dl = B(2\pi r) 
] 
Step 3: Determine the enclosed current. If the toroid has $N$ total turns and carries current $I$, the loop encloses all $N$ turns:
[ 
I_{\text{enc}} = NI 
] 
Step 4: Apply Ampere's Law:
[ 
B(2\pi r) = \mu_0 NI 
] 
Therefore:
[ 
\boxed{ 
B = \frac{\mu_0 NI}{2\pi r} 
} 
] 
Let $n = \frac{N}{2\pi r}$ be the number of turns per unit length. Then:
[ 
\boxed{ 
B = \mu_0 n I 
} 
] 
Observation 
* The magnetic field inside the core of a toroid is uniform.
* The magnetic field in the open space interior to ($r < a$) or exterior to ($r > b$) the toroid is $\boxed{0}$ because the net enclosed current is zero.

Chapter 11: Magnetic Materials (Susceptibility & Permeability)
===========================================================================

Syllabus Weightage: ⭐⭐⭐⭐ Highly repetitive short notes questions.

Why Do We Need Magnetization Vector (M)?
When a material is placed in an external magnetic field, its atomic dipoles align, creating an induced magnetic dipole moment. Magnetization measures this alignment.

Definition of Magnetization (M)
The magnetic dipole moment per unit volume of the material.
[ 
\boxed{ 
\vec M = \frac{\vec m_{\text{net}}}{V} 
} 
] 
Unit: Ampere per meter ($\boxed{ A/m }$)

Magnetic Intensity (H)
The external magnetic field applied, independent of the medium's magnetic properties.
[ 
\boxed{ 
\vec H = \frac{\vec B_0}{\mu_0} 
} 
] 
Unit: Ampere per meter ($\boxed{ A/m }$)

Total Magnetic Field (B) in a Medium
The total magnetic field inside a material is the sum of the external field ($\mu_0 H$) and the field due to magnetization ($\mu_0 M$):
[ 
\boxed{ 
\vec B = \mu_0(\vec H + \vec M) 
} 
] 

Magnetic Susceptibility ($\chi_m$)
Measures how easily a material magnetizes in response to an applied field:
[ 
\boxed{ 
\vec M = \chi_m \vec H 
} 
] 
$\chi_m$ is a dimensionless constant.

Magnetic Permeability ($\mu$)
The ratio of total magnetic field $B$ to magnetic intensity $H$: $\mu = B / H$.
Relative Permeability ($\mu_r$) is:
[ 
\boxed{ 
\mu_r = \frac{\mu}{\mu_0} 
} 
] 

Derivation: Relation Between $\mu_r$ and $\chi_m$
We know:
[ 
B = \mu_0(H + M) 
] 
Substitute $M = \chi_m H$:
[ 
B = \mu_0(H + \chi_m H) = \mu_0 H (1 + \chi_m) 
] 
We also know $B = \mu H$. Therefore:
[ 
\mu H = \mu_0 H (1 + \chi_m) 
] 
Cancel $H$:
[ 
\mu = \mu_0 (1 + \chi_m) \quad \implies \quad \boxed{ \mu_r = 1 + \chi_m } 
} 
] 
Proved. (Asked repeatedly in Set 2)

Classification of Magnetic Materials

1. Diamagnetic Materials
* Magnetize weakly in the opposite direction of the external field.
* Magnetic Susceptibility: $\chi_m$ is negative and small ($\chi_m < 0$).
* Relative Permeability: $\mu_r < 1$.
* Examples: Copper (Cu), Bismuth (Bi), Water ($H_2O$), Silicon (Si).

2. Paramagnetic Materials
* Magnetize weakly in the direction of the external field.
* Magnetic Susceptibility: $\chi_m$ is positive and small ($\chi_m > 0$).
* Relative Permeability: $\mu_r > 1$.
* Examples: Aluminium (Al), Sodium (Na), Platinum (Pt), Oxygen ($O_2$).

3. Ferromagnetic Materials
* Magnetize strongly in the direction of the external field.
* Magnetic Susceptibility: $\chi_m$ is positive and extremely large ($\chi_m \gg 1$).
* Relative Permeability: $\mu_r \gg 1$.
* Examples: Iron (Fe), Cobalt (Co), Nickel (Ni).

Comparison Table (Highly Visual Summary)
┌──────────────────────┬──────────────────────┬──────────────────────┬──────────────────────┐
│ Property             │ Diamagnetic          │ Paramagnetic         │ Ferromagnetic        │
├──────────────────────┼──────────────────────┼──────────────────────┼──────────────────────┤
│ Susceptibility (χ)   │ Negative (-10⁻⁵)     │ Small Positive (10⁻⁵)│ Very Large (> 10³)   │
│ Permeability (μ_r)   │ μ_r < 1              │ μ_r > 1              │ μ_r ≫ 1              │
│ In External Field    │ Weakly repelled      │ Weakly attracted     │ Strongly attracted   │
│ Temperature Effect   │ Independent of T     │ χ ∝ 1/T (Curie Law)  │ Decreases with T     │
└──────────────────────┴──────────────────────┴──────────────────────┴──────────────────────┘

Chapter 12: Electromagnetic Induction (EMI)
===========================================================================

PYQ Frequency 
✅ Set 2 → Self-Inductance of a Solenoid (5 Marks) 
✅ Set 3 → Faraday & Lenz's Law Conceptual (3 Marks) 

What is Electromagnetic Induction?
The phenomenon of generating an electromotive force (emf) and current in a circuit by changing the magnetic flux linked with it.

Faraday's Laws of EMI

Faraday's First Law
Whenever the magnetic flux linked with a circuit changes, an emf is induced in it. This induced emf lasts only as long as the change in magnetic flux continues.

Faraday's Second Law
The magnitude of the induced emf is directly proportional to the rate of change of magnetic flux linked with the circuit.
[ 
\boxed{ 
e = -N \frac{d\phi_B}{dt} 
} 
] 
where $N$ is the number of turns. The negative sign represents the direction of induced emf.

Lenz's Law & Conservation of Energy
Lenz's Law Statement: The direction of the induced current is always such that it opposes the change in magnetic flux that produces it.

Why is Lenz's Law a consequence of Conservation of Energy?
If we move the North pole of a magnet toward a coil:
* The coil develops a North pole on the facing side to repel the incoming magnet.
* We must do mechanical work against this repulsive force to push the magnet closer.
* This mechanical work is converted into the electrical energy of the induced current.
* If the opposite occurred (attraction), the magnet would accelerate indefinitely without any external work, violating the Law of Conservation of Energy! ❌

Motional emf
Consider a straight conductor of length $l$ moving with velocity $v$ perpendicular to a uniform magnetic field $B$ pointing into the page.

Diagram 
        ×   ×   ×   ×   ×   ×   ×   ×
        × ┌──────────┐  v →     ×   ×
        × │Conductor │ ───►     ×   ×  B (into page)
        × │Length l  │          ×   ×
        × └──────────┘          ×   ×
        ×   ×   ×   ×   ×   ×   ×   ×

Derivation
Magnetic flux enclosed by the moving circuit of width $x$:
[ 
\phi_B = Blx 
] 
According to Faraday's Law:
[ 
e = -\frac{d\phi_B}{dt} = -\frac{d}{dt}(Blx) = -Bl\left(\frac{dx}{dt}\right) 
] 
Since the width $x$ is decreasing, $\frac{dx}{dt} = -v$. Substituting:
[ 
\boxed{ 
e = Blv 
} 
] 
Proved.

Self-Induction & Mutual Induction

Self-Induction
The induction of an opposing emf in a single coil when the current passing through it changes.
Flux linkage is proportional to current: $N\phi_B = LI$.
Self-Induced emf:
[ 
\boxed{ 
e = -L \frac{dI}{dt} 
} 
] 
where $L$ is the Self-Inductance (Unit: Henry, H).

Derivation: Self-Inductance of a Long Solenoid (DU Set 2 Repeated)
Consider a long solenoid of length $l$, area $A$, and total turns $N$.
1. Magnetic field inside: $B = \mu_0 \frac{N}{l} I$
2. Flux through one turn: $\phi = BA = \mu_0 \frac{N}{l} I A$
3. Total flux linkage: $N\phi = \mu_0 \frac{N^2 A}{l} I$
4. Since $N\phi = LI$:
[ 
\boxed{ 
L = \frac{\mu_0 N^2 A}{l} 
} 
] 
Proved.

Mutual Induction
The induction of an emf in a secondary coil when the current flowing in a primary coil changes.
Flux linkage in secondary: $N_2 \phi_2 = MI_1$.
Mutual Induced emf:
[ 
\boxed{ 
e_2 = -M \frac{dI_1}{dt} 
} 
] 
where $M$ is the Mutual Inductance.

For two coaxial solenoids, Mutual Inductance is:
[ 
\boxed{ 
M = \frac{\mu_0 N_1 N_2 A}{l} 
} 
]

Chapter 13: Maxwell's Equations and Displacement Current
===========================================================================

PYQ Frequency: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ Direct 10-Mark Question in almost all sets.

Continuity Equation (Conservation of Charge)
Statement: The net rate of charge flowing out of a closed volume must equal the rate of decrease of charge within that volume.
1. Total charge inside volume $V$: $Q = \int_V \rho dV$
2. Outward current flowing through closed surface $S$: $I = \oint_S \vec{J} \cdot d\vec{A}$
3. By charge conservation: $I = -\frac{dQ}{dt}$
[ 
\oint_S \vec{J} \cdot d\vec{A} = -\frac{d}{dt}\int_V \rho dV = \int_V -\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t} dV 
] 
4. Applying Gauss's Divergence Theorem to the left side:
[ 
\int_V (\nabla\cdot\vec{J}) dV = \int_V -\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t} dV 
] 
Equating the integrands:
[ 
\boxed{ 
\nabla\cdot\vec{J} + \frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t} = 0 
} 
] 
This is the Continuity Equation. (Set 2 direct derivation)

Displacement Current
Why did Maxwell modify Ampere's Law?
Consider a parallel plate capacitor being charged by a current $I$.
* Apply Ampere's Law to a flat circular loop enclosing the wire: $\oint \vec{B}\cdot d\vec{l} = \mu_0 I$.
* Apply Ampere's Law to a balloon-shaped surface passing between the plates: $\oint \vec{B}\cdot d\vec{l} = 0$ (no wire passes through this surface).
* This is a logical contradiction! Maxwell resolved this by asserting that the changing electric field between the plates induces a magnetic field, acting like a current.

Definition of Displacement Current ($I_d$)
[ 
\boxed{ 
I_d = \epsilon_0 \frac{d\phi_E}{dt} 
} 
] 
where $\phi_E = EA = \frac{Q}{\epsilon_0 A} A = \frac{Q}{\epsilon_0}$ is the electric flux between the plates.

Modified Ampere's Law (Ampere-Maxwell Law)
[ 
\boxed{ 
\oint \vec B \cdot d\vec l = \mu_0(I + I_d) = \mu_0 I + \mu_0 \epsilon_0 \frac{d\phi_E}{dt} 
} 
] 
Differential Form:
[ 
\boxed{ 
\nabla \times \vec B = \mu_0 \vec J + \mu_0 \epsilon_0 \frac{\partial \vec E}{\partial t} 
} 
] 

Maxwell's Four Equations Summary (The Crown Jewel of E&M)
┌──────────────────────┬──────────────────────────────────────────┬──────────────────────────────────────────┐
│ Name of Equation     │ Differential Form                        │ Integral Form                            │
├──────────────────────┼──────────────────────────────────────────┼──────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ Gauss's Law (Elec)   │ ∇·E = ρ/ε₀                               │ ∮ E·dA = Q_enc/ε₀                        │
│ Gauss's Law (Mag)    │ ∇·B = 0                                  │ ∮ B·dA = 0                               │
│ Faraday's Law        │ ∇×E = -∂B/∂t                             │ ∮ E·dl = -d𝜙_B/dt                        │
│ Ampere-Maxwell Law   │ ∇×B = μ₀J + μ₀ε₀∂E/∂t                    │ ∮ B·dl = μ₀I + μ₀ε₀d𝜙_E/dt               │
└──────────────────────┴──────────────────────────────────────────┴──────────────────────────────────────────┘

Physical Significance of Maxwell's Equations
1. **Gauss's Law (Elec):** Electric charges are the sources of electric fields. Isolated electric charges exist.
2. **Gauss's Law (Mag):** Magnetic monopoles do not exist. Magnetic field lines always form closed loops.
3. **Faraday's Law:** A changing magnetic field produces a curl-like (non-conservative) electric field.
4. **Ampere-Maxwell Law:** Magnetic fields are produced by conduction currents and changing electric fields.

Chapter 14: Network Theorems (DU Set 1, 2 & 3 Core)

PYQ Connection

✅ Set 2 → Maximum Power Transfer Theorem Proof (7 Marks) 
✅ Set 3 → Norton's Theorem Numerical (5 Marks) 

Ohm's Law & Power
[ 
V = IR \quad \text{and} \quad P = VI = I^2 R = \frac{V^2}{R} 
] 

Kirchhoff's Laws

1. Kirchhoff's Current Law (KCL)
Statement: The sum of currents entering a junction must equal the sum of currents leaving the junction.
[ 
\boxed{ 
\sum I = 0 
} 
] 
Conservation Basis: Based on the **Law of Conservation of Charge**.

2. Kirchhoff's Voltage Law (KVL)
Statement: The algebraic sum of all potential differences and emfs around any closed loop is zero.
[ 
\boxed{ 
\sum V = 0 
} 
] 
Conservation Basis: Based on the **Law of Conservation of Energy**.

Thevenin's Theorem
Statement: Any active linear bilateral two-terminal network containing voltage sources and resistances can be replaced by a single equivalent voltage source $V_{\text{th}}$ in series with a single resistance $R_{\text{th}}$.

Diagrams 
        Original Active Network                Thevenin Equivalent
          ┌──────────────┐                     Series Circuit
          │ Linear       │ A                     R_th
      o───│ Active       │───o A             o───/\/\/\───o A
          │ Network      │                   │
      o───│              │───o B           (V_th)
          └──────────────┘                   │            
                                             o────────────o B

How to Find:
1. **$V_{\text{th}}$:** The open-circuit voltage across terminals A and B after removing the load resistor $R_L$.
2. **$R_{\text{th}}$:** The equivalent resistance looking back into terminals A and B with all independent voltage sources shorted and current sources opened.
Load Current:
[ 
\boxed{ 
I_L = \frac{V_{\text{th}}}{R_{\text{th}} + R_L} 
} 
] 

Norton's Theorem
Statement: Any active linear bilateral two-terminal network can be replaced by an equivalent circuit containing a single constant current source $I_N$ in parallel with a single resistance $R_N$.

Diagram 
        Norton Equivalent Parallel Circuit
                  A
         o────────┬────────o A
         │        │
       ( I_N)    [ R_N]  (Parallel)
         │        │
         o────────┴────────o B
                  B

Conversion between Thevenin and Norton:
[ 
\boxed{ 
R_N = R_{\text{th}} \quad \text{and} \quad I_N = \frac{V_{\text{th}}}{R_{\text{th}}} 
} 
] 

Maximum Power Transfer Theorem
Statement: Maximum power is transferred from a source network to a load resistor $R_L$ when the load resistance equals the internal series resistance $R_{th}$ of the source.

Derivation (DU Set 2 Repeated)
Power delivered to the load:
[ 
P = I_L^2 R_L = \left(\frac{V_{\text{th}}}{R_{\text{th}} + R_L}\right)^2 R_L = \frac{V_{\text{th}}^2 R_L}{(R_{\text{th}} + R_L)^2} 
] 
To find the value of $R_L$ that maximizes $P$, differentiate $P$ with respect to $R_L$ and set to $0$:
[ 
\frac{dP}{dR_L} = V_{\text{th}}^2 \left[ \frac{(R_{\text{th}} + R_L)^2(1) - R_L(2)(R_{\text{th}} + R_L)}{(R_{\text{th}} + R_L)^4} \right] = 0 
] 
Set numerator to 0:
[ 
(R_{\text{th}} + R_L)^2 - 2R_L(R_{\text{th}} + R_L) = 0 
] 
Divide by $(R_{\text{th}} + R_L)$:
[ 
R_{\text{th}} + R_L - 2R_L = 0 \quad \implies \quad \boxed{ R_L = R_{\text{th}} } 
] 
Proved.

Maximum Power Output Formula
Substituting $R_L = R_{\text{th}}$ back into the power equation:
[ 
P_{\text{max}} = \frac{V_{\text{th}}^2 R_{\text{th}}}{(R_{\text{th}} + R_{\text{th}})^2} = \frac{V_{\text{th}}^2 R_{\text{th}}}{4 R_{\text{th}}^2} = \boxed{ \frac{V_{\text{th}}^2}{4 R_{\text{th}}} } 
] 

Efficiency at Maximum Power Transfer
[ 
\eta = \frac{P_{\text{load}}}{P_{\text{total}}} = \frac{I^2 R_L}{I^2(R_{\text{th}} + R_L)} = \frac{R_{\text{th}}}{2 R_{\text{th}}} = \boxed{ 50\% } 
] 
Observation: At maximum power transfer, $50\%$ of the energy is lost as heat inside the source network!
